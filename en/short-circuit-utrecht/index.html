Short circuit or a circuit that keeps tripping? How to tell the difference

A circuit that trips can mean two things: overloading (too many appliances at once) or a short circuit (a real fault in an appliance or cable). You can tell the difference by when and how it trips.

Safety first

Do you see a black or melted spot at a socket, can you smell burning or did you hear a bang? Leave that circuit off, stop using the socket and call us. If there is smoke or fire: 112.

What to do now

  1. Does it trip after a while, when a lot is switched on?

    That is usually overloading. Switch off a few heavy appliances (kettle, microwave, heater, air fryer) and switch the circuit breaker back up. Does it happen often? Then you need an extra circuit.

  2. Does it trip as soon as you switch it on?

    That points to a short circuit. Unplug everything on that circuit and switch the circuit breaker up once.

  3. Does it stay up without appliances?

    Plug the appliances back in one by one. The appliance that makes the circuit trip is faulty: stop using it.

  4. Does it trip even without appliances?

    Then the short circuit is in the cable, a socket or a switch. Leave the circuit off and call us. Switching it back on repeatedly can cause damage and a fire risk.

Never work on the fuse box or live wiring yourself. Switching a breaker up or down and unplugging appliances is safe; leave all other repairs to a certified electrician.

Typical causes

Faulty lead or plug

A damaged, kinked or old lead. Often visible as discolouration.

Loose connection in a socket

A connection that gets hot and eventually short-circuits.

Drilling through a cable

After putting up a shelf or a picture: a classic.

Too much on one circuit

Lots of kitchen appliances or an electric heater on the same circuit.
